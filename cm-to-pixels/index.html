Centimetres to pixels for photos and signatures

Exam notices often give a size in centimetres. A file is measured in pixels. This page turns one into the other.

The rule in one line

Pixels = centimetres ÷ 2.54 × dots per inch. There are 2.54 centimetres in an inch, and "dots per inch" says how many pixels sit along each inch when the picture is printed.

Which dpi should I choose?

If the notice names one, use that. IBPS and SBI, for example, name 200 dpi and also give the pixels (240 × 240 for a 3 cm thumb impression), so there is nothing to work out.

If the notice gives only centimetres, any dpi gives the right shape. A higher dpi means more pixels and a sharper picture, but also a bigger file. For small KB limits such as 10 to 20 KB, 200 dpi is a sensible middle. What matters to the form is the shape, the file size and that the picture is clear.

Why a form may reject a "correct" size

A picture can be the right number of centimetres on paper and still fail, because the form checks the file: its type (JPG), its weight in KB and sometimes its pixels. Resize the file, not the printout. The resize tool does all three checks for you.

Working it out by hand

Take a passport photo of 3.5 cm by 4.5 cm at 200 dpi. Width: 3.5 ÷ 2.54 = 1.378 inches, and 1.378 × 200 = 276 pixels. Height: 4.5 ÷ 2.54 = 1.772 inches, and 1.772 × 200 = 354 pixels. So the file is 276 × 354 pixels, rounded to the nearest whole pixel.

When a notice gives millimetres, move the decimal point one place first: 35 mm × 45 mm is 3.5 cm × 4.5 cm. When it gives inches, there is nothing to divide: 2 inches at 300 dpi is simply 600 pixels.

From pixels back to centimetres

Centimetres = pixels ÷ dots per inch × 2.54. A file of 240 × 240 pixels printed at 200 dpi is 1.2 inches, about 3 cm, on each side. The same file printed at 100 dpi would be about 6 cm, and at 300 dpi only about 2 cm. That is why pixels alone do not tell you a printed size: the same picture can be printed large or small.

Shape matters more than the number

What cannot be changed without harm is the proportion of width to height. A 3.5 × 4.5 photo is a little taller than it is wide, in the ratio 7 to 9. If your picture is squeezed into a frame of another shape, faces look thin or wide. Crop the picture to the right shape first, then set the pixels. The resize tool crops for you: what you see in its frame is what is saved.

Common questions

The notice gives pixels and centimetres both. Which do I follow? The pixels, because that is what an upload form can measure. The centimetres tell you how the picture is meant to look when printed.

Does a higher dpi make my photo better? Only if the camera picture had the detail to begin with. Blowing up a small picture adds pixels but no sharpness, and makes the file heavier.

My phone photo is thousands of pixels wide. Is that a problem? No. Start with the large picture and make it smaller. Going down in size keeps it sharp; going up does not.
