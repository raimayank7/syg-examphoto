KB, pixels, cm and dpi: what an exam form is really asking for

Forms mix four different measures and rarely explain them. Once you see what each one measures, the rules stop looking strange.

Pixels: how many dots the picture has

A digital picture is a grid of tiny coloured dots called pixels. "200 × 230 pixels" means 200 dots across and 230 dots down. More pixels means more detail.

KB: how heavy the file is

KB (kilobyte) measures how much storage the file takes. It is not a size you can see. Two pictures with the same pixels can have very different KB, because a JPG can be saved at high or low quality. Lower quality throws away fine detail and makes the file lighter.

A photo straight from a phone camera is usually 2,000 to 5,000 KB. Exam forms want 20 to 200 KB. So the picture must be made smaller in pixels and saved at a suitable quality.

Centimetres: the size on paper

Centimetres describe a printed photo, such as the 3.5 cm by 4.5 cm passport size. A file has no centimetres of its own. When a notice gives centimetres for an upload, read it as the shape of the picture: 3.5 by 4.5 means a little taller than wide; 6 by 2 means a wide strip.

dpi: the bridge between the two

dpi means dots per inch: how many pixels are printed along one inch (2.54 cm). It converts paper size to pixels:

pixels = centimetres ÷ 2.54 × dpi

At 200 dpi, 3 cm becomes 236 pixels. IBPS rounds this to 240 for its 3 cm thumb impression. The cm to pixels page works this out for any size.

Why forms give a KB range and not one number

The largest KB keeps the exam body's storage and upload times under control. The smallest KB most likely works as a rough check on quality: a photo of only 2 KB is usually too blurred to recognise a face. Staying inside the range satisfies both.

How to change each one

  • Too many KB: reduce the pixels first, then lower the JPG quality a little.
  • Too few KB: start again from the original picture and save at higher quality. Do not enlarge a small, blurred picture; it stays blurred.
  • Wrong shape: crop. Never stretch or squeeze a face to fit.
  • Wrong type: save as JPG. PNG, HEIC and PDF are different file types.
